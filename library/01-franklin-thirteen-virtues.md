---
title: Franklin's thirteen virtues, and his little book
source: Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography (1791), public domain
---
Franklin, at about twenty, decided to attempt what he called moral perfection. He made a list of thirteen virtues, each with a short precept:

1. Temperance. Eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation.
2. Silence. Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation.
3. Order. Let all your things have their places; let each part of your business have its time.
4. Resolution. Resolve to perform what you ought; perform without fail what you resolve.
5. Frugality. Make no expense but to do good to others or yourself; i.e., waste nothing.
6. Industry. Lose no time; be always employed in something useful; cut off all unnecessary actions.
7. Sincerity. Use no hurtful deceit; think innocently and justly, and, if you speak, speak accordingly.
8. Justice. Wrong none by doing injuries, or omitting the benefits that are your duty.
9. Moderation. Avoid extremes; forbear resenting injuries so much as you think they deserve.
10. Cleanliness. Tolerate no uncleanliness in body, clothes, or habitation.
11. Tranquillity. Be not disturbed at trifles, or at accidents common or unavoidable.
12. Chastity. Rarely use venery but for health or offspring, never to dullness, weakness, or the injury of your own or another's peace or reputation.
13. Humility. Imitate Jesus and Socrates.

His method is the interesting part. He kept a small book with a page for each virtue, ruled into the days of the week, and each evening marked a black spot for every fault of the day. He worked on one virtue at a time for a week, then the next, so that a course took thirteen weeks and he could go through it four times a year. He admits he never reached perfection, and that Order in particular defeated him all his life; but he says that by the attempt he was a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been.

What is worth noticing: he did not try to be good in general. He picked one thing, watched himself for a week, and wrote down where he fell short. A reflection is that little book.

Question for you: if you kept one page for one week, which virtue would it be, and what would the black spots be for?
